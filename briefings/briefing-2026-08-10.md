## Macro backdrop

**The July jobs report (released Friday Aug 7, 8:30am ET) was a genuine outlier to the downside.** Nonfarm payrolls fell **−23,000** against a consensus around +80-83K — an outright decline, not just a soft gain — while the unemployment rate ticked down to **4.1%** (from June), mostly because people left the labor force rather than found jobs; average hourly earnings rose just 3.2% YoY, the slowest 12-month pace since May 2021. Prior months were revised sharply lower: May cut from +129K to +63K, June from +57K to +20K, a combined ~103,000 downgrade that leaves trailing 12-month average job growth at roughly 34,000/month. Government (−53K, mostly local education) and Leisure & Hospitality (−40K) led the losses; Construction and Private Education & Health Services were the bright spots. ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/08/07/jobs-report-july-2026.html), [Indeed Hiring Lab](https://www.hiringlab.org/2026/08/07/july-2026-jobs-report-unexpected-turbulence/))

**Why a bad jobs number produced a stock-market record**: the framing that matters this cycle is different from the "weak data = rate cuts coming" pattern of recent years. Under new Fed Chair **Kevin Warsh** (sworn in May 2026, replacing Jerome Powell), the FOMC has leaned hawkish on persistent tariff/energy-driven inflation — the June dot plot had 9 of 18 officials projecting at least one 2026 rate *hike*, and the July meeting held rates at 3.50-3.75% with 3 of 12 voting members dissenting in favor of a hike. Going into Friday's report, markets had priced September hike odds above 57%. The weak payrolls print was read as removing near-term hike risk, not as a growth scare — hence stocks rallying on bad news.
